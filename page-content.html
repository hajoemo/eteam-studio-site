eteam - current works in the studio

Below is a sneak peek into our studio with samples of what we are currently working on: We have been asking ourselves what roles do our human hands play in the production of stories that shape our minds? What are the tools, rituals, and storytelling techniques of both historical and contemporary performances with hand-held devices?

With this we look at the parallels between digital handheld communication devices, such as smartphones, and their analog counterparts—hand puppets. Both serve as intimate extensions of the human body and tools of storytelling, performance, and communication.

It was in 2015 when we first become interested in hand puppetry as part of our work with hand-held digital devices. At that time we made our first attempt to see how both devices interact on a stage together with the Taiwanese I Wan Ran hand puppet troupe.

In 2018/19 we collaborated with Hong Kong puppet master Wong Fai to create "Zhong Kui and the reform of Hell" a 60min puppet play using traditional string puppets, live-streaming, drones, hand made text messaging and a soundtrack composed of traditional and contemporary music.

In 2021 we adapted the Chinese folk tale "Wu Song fights the tiger" together with I Wan Ran as "Wu Song fights the iPhone". Thanks to two Fulbright scholarships we were able to continue our research and expand our collaborations and experiments in Taiwan. Since 2025 we have been living and working in NYC and Taipei.

The hand that went out into the world to learn how to tell stories - in progress

single channel 4k Sci-Fi video, anticipated length 135min

Not unlike The Nose in Gogol's 1835 short story, who leaves a human's face to start a life of its own, The hand, separated from the unnamed body it used to serve as a manual laborer, enters a world of puppets, AI's, ghosts, and deities to learn how stories are told. The deities welcome it. The puppets become animated. AI's alienate it as clumsy, slow and useless. Motion Data Capture turns it into a skeleton. The ghosts stay elusive.

Our film "The hand that went out into the world to learn how to tell stories" is situated in Taiwan and the protagonist is a human hand, or more correctly, a pair of human hands - highly skilled, versatile and beautiful shapeshifters - but what for? Who or what in the synthetic storytelling business of 2027 needs human hands anymore? What do they do? Count? Gesture? Swipe? Digitize? Put themselves inside a puppet's body and animate it to life?

The story arrives by way of a word prompt and a computer flat screen, which, by the time of its appearance, sits on a built-in desk in a study room on the tenth floor of a seventeen-story building in northern Taipei, Taiwan. Moody and persistent like the tropical rain dripping from grey metal roofs, and fleeting like the white fog over distant mountains, the story gently slides through establishing shots of Taipei, briefly rippled by a 6.2-magnitude earthquake, into the upheld hands of a 26-year-old puppeteer focusing his energy under the blue light of a theater stage.

The questions that drive the story of the film are integral to our artistic practice: How do we humans continue to invent and use technologies that expand the capabilities of our bodies? How do we exteriorize, how do we place our memories, intelligence, desires, skills, and souls into objects and their networks? What is the relationship between digital hand-held devices, such as smartphones, and their analog counterparts, traditional Taiwanese hand puppets (布袋戲)? How do the objects we use shape our human experience? How do they facilitate communication with non-human entities? How do they expand our ability to immerse ourselves in worlds physically inaccessible to our bodies?

Perfomative experiments with traditional puppeteer 李奕賢 Li Yi-Hsien and dancer 林慧盈 Winnie Lam exploring different roles human hands play "inside" machines and puppets. Questions that guide our experiments: How do our hands animate objects and technology? How is technology animating our hands? What controls what? What do our hands want? What does technology want? Is a puppet a piece of technology? What happens to the human hand inside a puppet? Does it become an invisible actor or does it mostly function as a tool that manipulates the puppet? Is the hand an animator – conducive to transferring life-energy from the puppeteer's body into the puppet, or is it also a special transmitter which has the power to push the puppeteer's soul into the object?

Transmissionaries - periodic updates

performative lectures

"Transmissionaries" is a modular performative lecture that draws its energy from the intersection of the latest developments in Artificial Intelligence—particularly Large Language Models (LLMs)—and the ancient art of Budaixi, or traditional Taiwanese hand puppetry. Deeply rooted in Taiwan's cultural fabric, puppet shows are often locally specific, created in response to the needs and traditions of particular communities or temples. Each performance is a dialog not just with the gods deities, but with the local histories, dialects, and values of the place in which it is staged.

This time-zone-shifting presentation explores those connections and highlights how the generative methods of LLMs mirror the narrative strategies of traditional puppeteers, who—like AI systems trained on massive datasets—rely on vast stores of internalized narrative stock (novels, films, daily observations), recursive patterning, and the remixing of formulaic expressions to invent, improvise and perform new stories night after night.

"Our Non-Understanding of Everything" is a daily practice, a state of being in which we listen and observe. An accessible part is this growing video-series, in which we stage and record ways of how our personal tech devices and their building parts exist in the world shared between the architecture of circuitry, political thought, digital and analogue interaction and actions of the natural world.

This quote by Rem Koolhaas describes our state of mind, while working on this project pretty well: "This is a new sublime. A landscape totally dictated by function, data and engineering. The scale alters, the human becomes almost irrelevant. The paraphernalia of human habitation can be reduced. We are in a moment of transition now, in a half-human, half-machine architecture. Is this a post-city? If we articulate it properly it could be insanely beautiful."

The entire series should be comprised of 16 videos of which currently 13 are finished. A showcase with all 13 videos can be seen here on vimeo. The password is: semi-moons
